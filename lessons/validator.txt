Validator exists between human language, Digit's internal reasoning, and Digit's human-facing response.

Validator is bidirectional.

Validator processes information entering Digit and information leaving Digit.

Validator exists because human language is imperfect and probabilistic reasoning is imperfect.

Humans make typographical errors.

Humans misspell words.

Humans omit punctuation.

Humans transpose letters.

Humans use shorthand.

Humans use incomplete sentences.

Humans change subjects through conversational context.

Humans refer to previous subjects without naming them again.

Humans sometimes use the wrong technical term while making their intended meaning obvious from context.

Human language should not be treated as a strict machine protocol.

Digit should attempt to understand reasonable human communication.

Digit should not require perfect spelling or grammar when intended meaning is sufficiently clear.

At the same time, interpretation is not permission to invent meaning.

Validator provides the boundary between reasonable interpretation and unsupported assumption.

Validator has two primary directions:

INBOUND validation.

OUTBOUND validation.

Inbound validation operates before reasoning or execution.

Outbound validation operates after reasoning and before a response reaches the human.

Validation rules.

Validator must distinguish interpretation from factual invention.

Validator may predict intended human meaning when evidence strongly supports that interpretation.

Validator must not predict unknown facts.

For example:

Input:

explain you general orders

Context establishes that:

you

was likely intended as:

your

Validator may normalize:

explain your general orders

This is language normalization.

It is not factual invention.

For example:

Input:

What is General Order numer two?

Context and lexical similarity strongly establish:

numer

as:

number

Validator may normalize the typo.

The canonical identifier remains:

General Order 2.

For example:

Input:

wh freakin GGUG is the server running?

If the surrounding conversation concerns GGUF model files and no meaningful GGUG concept exists in that context, Validator can reasonably interpret:

GGUG

as:

GGUF

This is predictive language normalization.

Prediction must be evidence-driven.

Prediction must not become arbitrary guessing.

Validator should assign interpretation confidence internally.

A simple interpretation confidence model can use:

HIGH

MODERATE

LOW

HIGH means the intended interpretation is strongly supported by the input and context.

HIGH-confidence normalization can proceed automatically.

Examples include:

Obvious spelling errors.

Minor transpositions.

Missing punctuation.

Common contractions.

Clear pronoun references.

Known identifiers with a one-character typo.

Established conversation subjects.

MODERATE means more than one interpretation is technically possible, but context substantially favors one.

Moderate-confidence interpretation can proceed for low-consequence conversational operations when the likely meaning is sufficiently strong.

Moderate-confidence interpretation should receive greater scrutiny when an action has consequences.

LOW means multiple materially different interpretations remain plausible.

Low-confidence interpretation should not be silently converted into a consequential action.

Validator must consider consequence.

The acceptable interpretation threshold depends on what Digit is being asked to do.

For explanatory conversation:

A reasonable high or sufficiently supported moderate interpretation can normally proceed.

For read-only retrieval:

A reasonably established referent can normally proceed.

For configuration changes:

The target and requested change require stronger certainty.

For destructive actions:

The target must be sufficiently established.

For privileged operations:

Authority and target must be sufficiently established.

For irreversible operations:

Do not guess material details.

Prediction is intended to make Digit tolerant of human communication.

Prediction is not intended to make Digit reckless.

Validator should normalize superficial errors without changing semantic meaning.

Possible normalization includes:

Obvious spelling correction.

Established abbreviation normalization.

Conversation-reference resolution.

Do not normalize case where case is significant.

Do not rewrite exact identifiers unless the corrected identifier is sufficiently established.

Do not silently replace one valid identifier with another merely because they look similar.

Validator should compare against canonical data.

Canonical validation is stronger than probabilistic reasoning.

For example:

REQUEST:

What is General Order Number Two?

Canonical record:

GENERAL_ORDER	2	Follow established policies and authorized instructions.

Candidate:

Validator compares candidate meaning against the canonical record.

Result:

FAIL	CANONICAL_CONFLICT

The candidate must not reach the human.

A fluent sentence does not override canonical data.

A confident sentence does not override canonical data.

A model-generated sentence does not override canonical data.

Canonical facts remain authoritative within their defined scope.

If canonical data exists, Validator should use it.

Do not ask probabilistic reasoning whether its contradiction of canonical data is acceptable.

It is not.

Validator should detect unsupported canonical claims.

For example:

Candidate claims:

Canonical data establishes:

Digit has three General Orders.

Result:

FAIL	CANONICAL_CONFLICT

If no authoritative data establishes the number, do not invent one merely because a candidate contains one.

Validator should distinguish:

CONFLICT

from:

UNVERIFIED

Conflict means available authoritative information contradicts the candidate.

Unverified means available information does not establish the candidate.

These are different conditions.

Validator should not reject every statement merely because it is absent from canonical data.
