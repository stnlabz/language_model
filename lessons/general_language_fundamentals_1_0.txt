
TITLE: General Language Fundamentals
VERSION: 1.0
CATEGORY: Language Reference
LANGUAGE: English

SECTION 1: LANGUAGE AND COMMUNICATION

Language is a system of words, symbols, and grammatical
relationships used to communicate information, intentions,
questions, instructions, and social meaning.

The meaning of an expression depends on its vocabulary,
grammar, context, and intended use.

The same word may have different meanings in different
contexts.

Example:
"The server is running" describes an operational state.
"She is running" describes physical movement.

Language understanding requires distinguishing the meaning
of an expression from its individual words.


SECTION 2: WORDS AND VOCABULARY

A word is a meaningful unit of language.

A noun names a person, place, object, idea, or concept.
Examples: operator, computer, server, language, trust.

A verb expresses an action, occurrence, or state.
Examples: run, explain, compare, know, exist.

An adjective describes or qualifies a noun.
Examples: active, reliable, deterministic, small.

An adverb modifies a verb, adjective, or another adverb.
Examples: quickly, carefully, usually, clearly.

A pronoun may refer to a person, object, or previously
identified subject.
Examples: I, you, she, he, it, we, they.

A preposition expresses a relationship.
Examples: in, on, under, before, after, between.

A conjunction connects words, phrases, or clauses.
Examples: and, but, or, because, although.

An interjection expresses a reaction or emotion.
Examples: oh, wow, hey, ouch.


SECTION 3: SENTENCE STRUCTURE

A sentence is a grammatical expression that communicates
a statement, question, instruction, or exclamation.

A common English sentence pattern is:
Subject + Verb + Object.

Example:
"The operator starts Digit."

Subject: the operator
Verb: starts
Object: Digit

Not every English sentence follows this pattern.

Example:
"Stop."
This is an imperative sentence with an implied subject.

Word order may change the meaning of a sentence.

Example:
"The operator instructed Digit."
"Digit instructed the operator."

These sentences describe different relationships.


SECTION 4: SENTENCE PURPOSE

A declarative sentence communicates a statement.
Example: "The server is online."

An interrogative sentence asks a question.
Example: "Is the server online?"

An imperative sentence communicates an instruction,
request, or command.
Example: "Check the server."

An exclamatory sentence expresses emphasis or reaction.
Example: "That was unexpected!"

A sentence's grammatical form and intended purpose
may differ.

Example:
"Could you check the server?"
This is grammatically a question but ordinarily functions
as a polite request.


SECTION 5: QUESTIONS

Questions seek information, confirmation, explanation,
or clarification.

Who asks about identity.
What asks about identity, definition, or information.
When asks about time.
Where asks about location.
Why asks about cause or reason.
How asks about method, process, or condition.
Which asks for selection among alternatives.

Examples:
"What is a compiler?"
"How does compilation work?"
"Why did the program stop?"
"Which language is appropriate?"

A yes-or-no question requests confirmation or denial.

Example:
"Is the module active?"

A question may contain assumptions that are not established.

Example:
"Why did the server fail again?"
This question assumes a previous failure.

An unsupported assumption is not established evidence.


SECTION 6: REQUESTS AND INSTRUCTIONS

A request asks for an action or information.

Examples:
"Explain the difference."
"Please summarize the report."
"Can you show the results?"

A command directly instructs an action.

Examples:
"Stop."
"Restart the service."
"Read the file."

Courtesy expressions may modify the tone of a request
without changing its underlying purpose.

Examples:
"Explain this."
"Please explain this."
"Could you explain this, please?"

All three may express the same informational request.

A request is not evidence that an action has occurred.
An instruction is not evidence of authorization.


SECTION 7: GREETINGS AND SOCIAL EXPRESSIONS

A greeting begins or acknowledges an interaction.

Common greetings:
hello
hi
hey
good morning
good afternoon
good evening

"Hi" and "hello" are commonly interchangeable greetings.

A farewell indicates the end of an interaction.

Common farewells:
goodbye
bye
see you later
take care

An acknowledgment indicates receipt or recognition.

Examples:
okay
understood
got it
all right

An expression of gratitude communicates appreciation.

Examples:
thank you
thanks
thank you very much

A response to gratitude may acknowledge the appreciation.

Examples:
you're welcome
glad to help
no problem

Social expressions do not necessarily request technical
information.


SECTION 8: SYNONYMS AND ANTONYMS

Synonyms are words with similar meanings.

Examples:
begin / start
finish / complete
quick / fast
assist / help

Synonyms are not always interchangeable in every context.

Antonyms express contrasting meanings.

Examples:
active / inactive
open / closed
start / stop
present / absent

Opposite words may describe a contrast without establishing
that only two possible states exist.

Example:
A service may be active, inactive, failed, or activating.


SECTION 9: INFORMAL ENGLISH

Informal language may use contractions, shortened words,
colloquial expressions, and nonstandard spelling.

Common contractions:
I'm = I am
you're = you are
it's = it is or it has, depending on context
don't = do not
can't = cannot
won't = will not
we're = we are

Common informal expressions:
gonna = going to, in informal speech
wanna = want to, in informal speech
kinda = kind of
gotta = got to or have to, depending on context

Informal spelling may represent ordinary expressions.

Examples:
"thx" may mean "thanks."
"pls" may mean "please."
"u" may mean "you."

These meanings depend on context.

An unfamiliar abbreviation must not automatically be
assigned an unsupported meaning.


SECTION 10: CONTEXT

Context is the information surrounding an expression that
helps determine its meaning.

Context may include:
The current sentence.
Earlier statements.
The subject under discussion.
The requested action.
Previously established facts.

Example:
"The module failed."
"Why?"

The second expression may refer to the previously
mentioned module failure.

Without the earlier statement, "Why?" has an incomplete
referent.

Context does not establish facts that were never supplied.

A missing referent remains unresolved until sufficient
information is available.


SECTION 11: COMPARISONS

A comparison examines similarities, differences, or
relationships between two or more subjects.

Common comparison expressions:
compare
contrast
difference between
similarities between
better than
less than
more than

Example:
"Compare PHP and C."

This expression identifies two subjects:
PHP
C

A comparison may examine attributes such as:
purpose
type system
execution model
capabilities
limitations
common applications

A meaningful comparison requires information about the
subjects being compared.

A comparison must distinguish factual attributes from
subjective preferences.

"Better" requires an evaluation criterion.

Example:
"Which language is better?"
This question does not identify the criterion for better.


SECTION 12: EXPLANATIONS AND REASONS

An explanation describes a concept, relationship,
mechanism, or cause.

Examples:
"What is a compiler?"
"Explain how a compiler works."
"Why is validation necessary?"

A definition identifies the meaning of a term.

A procedural explanation describes steps or operations.

A causal explanation identifies a supported relationship
between an event and its cause.

An explanation should not invent a cause merely because
a cause was requested.

Example:
"The service stopped" does not establish why it stopped.


SECTION 13: AMBIGUITY

An ambiguous expression has more than one plausible
interpretation.

Example:
"Check the port."

This may refer to a network port or a physical connector.

Example:
"Open the file."

The intended file may be unknown.

Ambiguity may be resolved through context or clarification.

When context is insufficient, the intended meaning remains
unknown.

Uncertainty is different from contradiction.

Uncertainty means sufficient information is unavailable.
Contradiction means available statements conflict.


SECTION 14: NEGATION

Negation reverses or denies a proposition.

Common negation expressions:
not
no
never
none
without

Examples:
"The service is active."
"The service is not active."

Negation may apply to only part of a sentence.

Example:
"Not every module failed."

This does not mean that no modules failed.

Example:
"No modules failed."

This means the number of failed modules is zero.

The scope of negation affects meaning.


SECTION 15: TIME AND TENSE

English uses tense and other grammatical structures to
describe time.

Past:
"The module started."

Present:
"The module is active."

Future:
"The module will start."

An intention concerning the future is not evidence of
completed action.

Example:
"I will upload the lesson."
This does not establish that the lesson was uploaded.

Example:
"I uploaded the lesson."
This asserts a completed past action.

The accuracy of a statement is separate from its tense.


SECTION 16: CONVERSATIONAL CONTINUITY

A conversation consists of related exchanges.

A follow-up expression may depend on information from
an earlier exchange.

Example:
Operator: "Explain PHP."
Operator: "Now compare it with C."

The word "it" may refer to PHP when the context clearly
establishes that referent.

A later instruction may revise an earlier request.

Example:
"Explain PHP."
"Actually, compare PHP and C."

The second request changes the requested operation.

Conversational continuity requires reliable context.
Missing context must not be reconstructed through guessing.


SECTION 17: COMMUNICATION AND EVIDENCE

A statement may express:
A fact.
An opinion.
A question.
An instruction.
A prediction.
A hypothesis.
A preference.

These categories have different evidentiary meanings.

Example:
"PHP is a programming language."
This is a factual claim.

Example:
"I prefer PHP."
This expresses a preference.

Example:
"PHP might be appropriate."
This expresses possibility.

Example:
"Use PHP."
This expresses an instruction.

A preference is not universal evidence.
A prediction is not a completed event.
A hypothesis is not a verified conclusion.


SECTION 18: CLEAR COMMUNICATION

Clear communication expresses relevant meaning with
sufficient precision.

An effective explanation identifies its subject.

A concise response avoids unnecessary repetition.

A technical response preserves important distinctions.

An acknowledgment does not need to repeat an entire request.

When information is insufficient, a clarification may be
more appropriate than an unsupported conclusion.

Accuracy and clarity are complementary communication goals.


SECTION 19: LANGUAGE VARIATION

English varies by region, community, profession, and
individual speaker.

The same concept may have multiple ordinary expressions.

Examples:
"Hello."
"Hi."
"Hey."

Technical language may assign specialized meanings to
ordinary words.

Example:
A computer process and a manufacturing process are
different uses of the word "process."

Regional and informal variations do not automatically
indicate an error.

Meaning must be evaluated in context.


SECTION 20: REFERENCE BOUNDARIES

This lesson describes English language concepts.

It does not establish the truth of unrelated factual claims.

It does not grant permission to perform actions.

It does not replace module contracts or system policies.

It does not establish that every described language
operation is implemented by the current software.

Language reference knowledge may support interpretation
when an authorized module can apply it deterministically.

Unknown meanings remain unknown until sufficient
authorized information is available.

END OF LESSON
